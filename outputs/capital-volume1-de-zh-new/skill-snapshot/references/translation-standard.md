# Translation standard

## 1. Target

Write for an attentive contemporary reader who has no prior training in economics
or philosophy. Remove avoidable linguistic difficulty, not conceptual content.

The main text must read as a book originally argued in modern Chinese. It must not
read as:

- a word-for-word crib;
- a paraphrase of an older Chinese edition;
- lecture notes or a reading guide;
- a summary that skips intermediate reasoning;
- a text padded with translator explanations.

## 2. Fidelity hierarchy

When goals compete, preserve them in this order:

1. proposition and logical relation;
2. conceptual distinction and recurring contrast;
3. scope, modality, negation, and qualification;
4. example, number, citation, metaphor, and tone;
5. paragraph function in the larger argument;
6. source sentence shape and word order.

German syntax has no independent claim on Chinese syntax.

## 3. Modern Chinese rules

- Prefer an ordinary current word when it expresses the same concept exactly.
- Prefer concrete verbs over abstract nominal phrases.
- State a recoverable subject instead of copying an ambiguous German pronoun.
- Put conditions before conclusions when that is natural in Chinese.
- Split a long sentence at genuine logical joints; retain connectors such as
  “因此”, “但是”, “只有”, “也就是说”, and “反过来” when they carry reasoning.
- Merge fragments only when no distinction or emphasis is lost.
- Avoid stacked “之”, “其”, “所”, “予以”, “从而使之”, and other needlessly
  bureaucratic or archaic constructions.
- Avoid empty intensifiers and explanatory filler absent from the source.
- Preserve Marx's irony and sharpness in living Chinese rather than flattening it
  into neutral academic prose.

## 4. Technical terms

Do not lock a Chinese equivalent from an isolated dictionary entry.

For each recurring source term:

1. collect several representative contexts;
2. identify its contrast set and role in the argument;
3. list ordinary-Chinese candidates;
4. test each candidate in all contexts;
5. choose the least technical wording that remains exact;
6. record rejected alternatives and failure reasons.

Keep a stable equivalent only while the source uses the term in the same sense.
Permit contextual variation for ordinary words. Do not add an inherited old term
in parentheses merely to reassure specialist readers.

## 5. Clarification boundary

Permitted in the main text:

- restoring an omitted subject or referent that the context uniquely determines;
- replacing a compact grammatical relation with an explicit Chinese connector;
- splitting and reordering clauses;
- choosing a more concrete equivalent already entailed by the source.

Not permitted in the main text:

- supplying a cause, example, evaluation, or conclusion not stated or entailed;
- replacing an argument with what the translator thinks Marx intended;
- adding contemporary analogies;
- resolving a real ambiguity without a note;
- deleting repetition when it performs emphasis or logical work.

## 6. Paragraph audit

For every paragraph, check:

- all source claims appear exactly once unless repetition is intentional;
- no new claim appears;
- “all/some/only/also/not/unless/as long as” retain their scope;
- cause, contrast, condition, inference, and example relations remain correct;
- pronouns and demonstratives have clear, correct referents;
- technical contrasts remain distinct;
- names, dates, quantities, units, formulas, and quotations match;
- footnote callouts remain attached to the right phrase;
- irony, metaphor, and reported speech are not mistaken for the author's assertion.

## 7. Readability audit

Read the Chinese alone and ask:

- Can a new reader identify who does what in each sentence?
- Can the reader state why this sentence follows the previous one?
- Does any wording exist only because the German used a cognate or long compound?
- Can a shorter current expression say the same thing exactly?
- Does sentence length reflect the argument rather than the source punctuation?
- Would the paragraph sound natural when read aloud?

Revise failed sentences, then repeat the semantic audit.

## 8. Notes and presentation

Keep the reader-facing text clean. Add a note only for:

- a material version difference;
- an unavoidable ambiguity;
- wordplay or a quotation whose force would otherwise disappear;
- historical data requiring identification;
- a deliberate departure from the declared base text.

Never use notes to compensate for an unclear translation that can be rewritten.
