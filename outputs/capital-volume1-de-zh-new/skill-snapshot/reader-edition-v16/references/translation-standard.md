# Plain-Chinese reader's-edition standard

## 1. Purpose and reader

This edition exists so that a reader with high-school or undergraduate education,
but no training in economics, philosophy, or Marxist terminology, can understand
the argument without a teacher beside them.

The final Chinese should read like a careful contemporary translation written
in natural Chinese. It is not an academic critical edition, a word-for-word
crib, a modernization of an older Chinese translation, a summary, or a separate
reading guide.

The decisive test is not “Can a specialist reconstruct every German expression?”
It is “Can a new reader say, after one reading, what the passage claims and what
move each sentence makes?” The reader need not find every premise proved,
resolve a question postponed by the source, or accept the theory.

## 2. Fidelity in content, freedom in Chinese expression

Stay close to the source in what it says, not in how German happens to phrase
it. Fidelity applies to claims, logical relations, conceptual distinctions,
examples, qualifications, historical detail, and argumentative order. It does
not give source-shaped nouns, word order, sentence length, repetition, or
technical-looking diction any priority over shorter natural Chinese.

Preserve by default:

- the order in which claims, qualifications, examples, and returns appear;
- the source paragraph boundaries;
- facts, names, figures, quotations, metaphors, and historically specific detail;
- the distinction between what the text asserts, assumes, infers, illustrates,
  and postpones.

Use the shortest direct Chinese that preserves those things. Apply this ladder:

1. **Translate directly into Chinese.** Change parts of speech, turn abstract
   nouns into actions, name the actual object, remove empty verbal padding, and
   choose the shortest ordinary wording that preserves the claim. This is normal
   translation, not free reconstruction.
2. **Reshape the sentence.** Split, combine, or locally reorder sentences inside
   the same source paragraph when ordinary Chinese requires it.
3. **Reconstruct the expression.** Use freer meaning-centered rewriting only
   when the first two levels still leave the passage genuinely opaque.

Level 3 is an exception, not the house style. Record its exact necessity in the
meaning review. Do not use it merely to make an argument more persuasive,
modern, concise, or theoretically complete.

Do not confuse fidelity with lexical resemblance. If “它有什么性质，以及它有
多少” preserves the same distinction more directly than “从质和量两方面来
看”, use the former. If “用处不能脱离实物存在” says the same thing more
plainly than “有用性并不是凭空存在”, use the former. Retain a technical term
only when the argument later depends on recognizing that term.

Do not repair the source's theory. If the source relies on a contested premise,
makes a compressed inference, or answers a question later, translate that move
clearly and faithfully. A short translator note may identify a likely
misreading, but it must not supply a new proof, import later concepts into the
main text, or turn the translation into a lecture.

## 3. What may and may not be sacrificed

When clarity conflicts with formal fidelity, preserve in this order:

1. the paragraph's core claim and role in the larger argument;
2. causal, conditional, contrastive, inferential, and quantitative relations;
3. distinctions between concepts that later reasoning depends on;
4. facts, examples, names, numbers, formulas, and quoted positions;
5. useful qualifications and rhetorical force;
6. repetition, metaphor, minor detail, source sentence shape, and word order.

The translator may routinely:

- replace pronouns with their actual referents;
- use natural Chinese word order and punctuation;
- split a German-shaped sentence into ordinary Chinese sentences;
- add the minimum connector needed to show a relation already present.

The translator may exceptionally, after lower-intervention options fail:

- locally reorder sentences within a source paragraph;
- turn a dense abstraction into a direct action;
- state an intermediate step securely entailed by the immediate source;
- shorten redundant repetition or replace an opaque metaphor;
- omit a minor stylistic detail whose retention would materially block
  comprehension.

Every exceptional intervention must be recorded. Do not omit evidence, examples,
historical particulars, or qualifications merely to shorten the text.

The source paragraph is the default unit of the final prose. Preserve its
boundary unless doing so would materially prevent one-read comprehension and
sentence-level rewriting cannot solve the problem. Do not split a source
paragraph merely to produce shorter blocks, vary visual rhythm, isolate an
example, or make an abstract passage look easier. Do not join adjacent source
paragraphs merely because they discuss the same topic.

A paragraph boundary may change only when the change has a specific reading
function, such as separating two independently developed arguments inside an
exceptionally long source paragraph, keeping a heading or displayed formula with
the prose it governs, or joining a source fragment that cannot stand as a
Chinese paragraph. Record every such change and its reason in the readability
review.

The translator may not:

- reverse, weaken, or strengthen the core claim;
- change who causes or conditions what;
- turn “some” into “all”, possibility into certainty, or description into praise;
- merge distinctions such as value/price, labour/labour-power, or
  product/commodity when the argument relies on them;
- invent a modern example, historical fact, motive, or conclusion and present it
  as Marx's own;
- conceal a genuine ambiguity behind a confident paraphrase;
- make a contested interpretation sound like an undisputed source statement.

If a deliberate simplification changes a meaningful but non-core detail, record
it in the internal decision log. The reader need not see the apparatus unless the
choice could mislead them.

## 4. Chinese prose rules

- Prefer words used in ordinary serious conversation today.
- Prefer the shortest sentence that preserves the same claim and emphasis.
- Prefer “东西、物品、用处、多少、怎样做” to “物、物体、有用性、数量规
  定、操作方式” unless the more technical expression carries a distinction the
  paragraph actually needs.
- Never keep an abstract source-shaped phrase merely because it sounds more
  exact. Ask what extra meaning it preserves. If the answer is “none”, replace
  it with the plainer alternative.
- Use direct subject–verb–object sentences whenever possible.
- Keep one main action or inference per sentence.
- Name the actor. Avoid long chains of “它、这、其、这种” with uncertain
  referents.
- Put the logical connector where the reader needs it: “因为”, “所以”, “但”,
  “只有”, “也就是说”, “换句话说”, “反过来”.
- A semicolon has no special theoretical function. Prefer a period plus the
  actual logical connector when two clauses perform separate steps. Keep a
  semicolon only when two grammatically complete clauses form a tight parallel
  or contrast and a period would make that relationship harder to see.
- Do not use semicolons to preserve German sentence length, compress a chain of
  reasoning, or create an academic tone. Apply a replacement test to every
  semicolon: if a period, comma, or explicit connector is equally clear, replace
  it.
- Prefer concrete verbs to phrases such as “对……进行……”“使……得以实现”.
- Replace inherited translationese unless the term is genuinely needed.
- Avoid literary archaism, bureaucratic phrasing, decorative synonyms, and
  German-shaped clause nesting.
- Preserve the source's speaking position. State its claims and definitions
  directly. Do not turn them into third-person commentary such as “马克思认为”,
  “作者指出”, or “马克思把……称为……” unless the source itself explicitly shifts
  to that viewpoint. Put genuine translator commentary in a translator note.
- Preserve sharpness and irony in living Chinese, not stiff academic prose.
- Read every paragraph aloud. If it cannot be spoken naturally, revise it.
- After every paragraph, write a private one-sentence plain paraphrase. Compare
  it with the draft. Any extra abstraction or repetition in the draft must
  justify itself by preserving a real source detail, distinction, or rhetorical
  effect.

Unacceptable:

> 所有那些通过劳动只是同土地脱离直接联系的东西，都是天然存在的劳动对象。

Acceptable:

> 有些劳动只是把现成的自然物从原来的环境中取出来，比如捕鱼、伐木和采矿。

The acceptable version changes the grammar and groups Marx's examples with the
claim, but makes the intended distinction immediately visible.

## 5. Argument continuity

Sentence-level clarity is not enough. A paragraph fails if every sentence is
individually understandable but the reader must reconstruct how they fit
together.

Before drafting a passage, write an internal argument spine:

1. What question is this passage answering?
2. What is the main object being discussed?
3. What work does each step perform: definition, mechanism, consequence,
   contrast, qualification, example, or return to the main claim?
4. Why does each step come next?

Then write the Chinese so that this spine is visible without the reader seeing
the outline.

- Keep the same conceptual subject through a chain of reasoning. If the focus
  must shift from a capacity to the person who possesses it, or from a product
  to its value, name the connection explicitly.
- Do not use two or three loosely related sentences where one sentence can show
  the definition, means, or consequence directly.
- Make the relation between adjacent sentences explicit when it is not already
  obvious in Chinese. Choose the actual relation—definition, explanation,
  cause, result, contrast, or qualification—instead of inserting a vague
  conjunction.
- Do not preserve an abstract umbrella phrase if it leaves a new reader asking
  what concretely remains the same. State the invariant process or claim in
  ordinary words; retain the technical abstraction only if later reasoning
  depends on it.
- Recast nested comparisons and multiple “the more … the more …” clauses into
  the natural Chinese order of condition, experience, and consequence.
- Avoid viewpoint oscillation. A passage should not jump among what the
  capitalist buys, what labour-power is, and what happens to the worker unless
  that movement is itself the argument.
- Do not add transitions merely for smoothness. Every bridge must express a
  relation entailed by the source.
- Do not turn a source-level theoretical gap into a translation-level failure.
  If the text asserts an inference, make the asserted relation visible. Do not
  add a proof the source does not give.
- When a paragraph sets aside several differences in order to isolate a common
  point, announce the scope of that move before listing the differences and
  return explicitly to the common point afterward. Do not leave the reader to
  infer at the end that several apparently independent sentences were all
  qualifications of one claim.

After drafting, run two paragraph-level tests:

- **Next-sentence test:** for every sentence after the first, can a new reader
  say why it follows from the previous one?
- **One-breath paraphrase test:** can the reader summarize the paragraph as one
  continuous thought rather than a list of separate statements?
- **Shorter-without-loss test:** can the same source content and logic be said in
  noticeably fewer or more ordinary words? If yes, revise. “Closer to the German
  wording” is not a reason to keep the longer version.

A semicolon, a long sentence, or a technical term is not inherently a defect.
Keep it when it makes the relation clearer than shorter alternatives.

### Paragraph architecture

- Start by drafting one Chinese paragraph for each source paragraph.
- Improve readability inside that boundary first: reorder sentences, replace
  pronouns, add explicit logical relations, and vary sentence length.
- Split only after identifying the exact point at which the original boundary
  blocks comprehension. Each resulting paragraph must still belong to the same
  continuous source argument.
- Join only when a source item is a heading, formula, fragment, or continuation
  that would be misleading as a standalone Chinese paragraph.
- In the readability review, list the source paragraphs whose boundaries
  changed. If none changed, state that source paragraphing was preserved.

## 6. Concepts and terminology

Use the least technical stable term that preserves a distinction needed by the
argument. A familiar word is better than inherited jargon when it works across
the relevant contexts.

For each recurring concept:

1. collect representative contexts;
2. identify the contrasts that must remain visible;
3. test plain-Chinese candidates;
4. choose a default and record its meaning, limits, and rejected alternatives;
5. allow contextual variation when the German word is ordinary rather than a
   fixed concept.

When a task encounters an important term with no approved decision, use the
clearest provisional wording in the draft, explain it naturally at first use if
needed, and list the proposal in the meaning review. It becomes a project-wide
default only after it is entered in the decision or terminology log.

Do not add an old Chinese term in parentheses merely for scholarly reassurance.
If a conventional term is needed for later recognition, introduce it naturally,
for example: “生产时使用的条件和工具（通常叫作生产资料）”. Do this only when
it helps the reader.

## 7. Inline translator notes

Use the exact form:

```text
〔译者注：……〕
```

A note is justified when it:

- prevents a predictable modern misunderstanding;
- identifies historical information the sentence assumes;
- distinguishes Marx's term from its ordinary present-day meaning;
- marks a real ambiguity or a consequential interpretive choice;
- gives a short contemporary analogy that illuminates, but does not replace, the
  argument.

Rules:

- Place the note immediately after the relevant phrase.
- Use one or two short sentences.
- Normally use no more than one note in a paragraph.
- State clearly what belongs to the translator.
- Do not use notes for ideological commentary, rebuttal, praise, or material that
  can be expressed cleanly in the translation itself.
- Delete any note that merely repairs bad Chinese; rewrite the Chinese instead.
- Do not use a note to answer every theoretical objection, preview a later
  chapter, defend Marx, or make an unproved step appear proved.

## 8. Meaning audit

For each task, compare the Chinese with the verified source and check:

- the core claim and every indispensable reasoning step remain;
- cause, condition, contrast, inference, negation, quantity, and time remain
  correct;
- key conceptual contrasts remain distinct;
- names, dates, units, formulas, quotations, and footnote attachment are correct;
- simplifications do not create a new factual or theoretical claim;
- additions are either entailed clarifications or clearly marked translator
  notes.
- paragraph-level bridges preserve the source's actual reasoning rather than
  supplying a new argument.
- every changed paragraph boundary has a concrete comprehension reason and does
  not merge claims that the source deliberately separated.
- every freer reconstruction identifies why ordinary source-near Chinese was
  insufficient;
- explanations do not import later theory or replace the source's argument with
  the translator's stronger one.

This is not a word-matching audit. Different wording is expected.

## 9. Source-blind readability audit

Read only the Chinese first. Do not use knowledge of the German or the intended
argument to supply a bridge that the Chinese itself does not communicate. Follow
the evidence format in `review-protocol.md`; a checklist without paragraph-level
evidence is not a review.

Ask about the Chinese:

- Can a first-time reader identify who is doing what?
- Can the reader explain the point of the paragraph in one sentence?
- Does each sentence visibly follow from the previous one?
- Does the conceptual subject stay stable, or is every necessary shift named?
- Can the paragraph be paraphrased as one continuous line of thought?
- Is any term used before the reader can understand it?
- Has any direct source statement been unnecessarily recast as a report about
  what “Marx” or “the author” says?
- Does any sentence need a second reading only because of its wording?
- Can a shorter, more ordinary expression say the same thing?
- Does any phrase such as “物质载体、共同实质、客观表现、质和量” preserve a
  distinction that a plainer phrase would lose? If not, it is a translation
  obstacle rather than a sign of rigor.
- Does each paragraph boundary normally match the source? If not, is the change
  necessary for comprehension rather than merely stylistic?
- Does every semicolon make a parallel or contrast clearer? If not, replace it
  with ordinary modern-Chinese sentence structure.
- If the paragraph excludes or postpones several differences, does it name the
  scope of the exclusion and explicitly return to the invariant claim?

Do not ask whether the claim is true, persuasive, fully proved, or consistent
with material that appears later. Distinguish three kinds of finding:

- **T — translation obstacle:** the Chinese hides the actor, relation, referent,
  scope, or meaning, or uses a longer abstract formulation without preserving
  additional meaning. This fails the draft.
- **A — authorial difficulty:** the reader understands the claim and its stated
  relation but finds the premise compressed, disputed, or unproved. This does
  not fail the translation.
- **D — deferred question:** the source intentionally answers the issue later.
  This does not fail the translation unless the Chinese falsely presents it as
  already settled.

Only unresolved T findings require revision. Any rewrite invalidates both review
hashes and returns through meaning review.

## 10. Final Markdown

Each chapter is one UTF-8 Markdown file:

```markdown
# 第五章 劳动过程和价值增殖过程

## 一、劳动过程

正文……

〔译者注：必要时才出现。〕
```

The released file contains only reader-facing headings, prose, quotations,
formulas, and sparse translator notes. Do not expose German text, paragraph IDs,
edition sigla, task IDs, status markers, review forms, or internal decisions.
